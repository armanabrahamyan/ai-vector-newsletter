A block of ink, opened in one direction

Six marks drawn, rasterised at true device pixels, and judged at 16 px before anyone was allowed to admire them at 200. Four survived the size, two did not. One is recommended. Nothing here is decided — the mark is the operator's to ratify.

The Open Block

The V is never drawn. It is the paper coming through — and its long arm keeps going until it takes the corner off.

How this was judged

Three things in this document are measurements rather than opinions, and they did most of the deciding.

The 16 px exam came first. Every candidate was rendered by a real browser at a device scale factor of 1 — actual 16 px pixels, not a shrunken vector — and then magnified with nearest-neighbour so the pixels could be inspected. Two candidates were killed by that image and two were redrawn because of it. The strip below is that raster, not a re-rendering.

The lockup ratio is measured, not chosen by eye. Newsreader 500 at the template's 32 px masthead size has a cap height of 21.81 px (measured in-browser with TextMetrics, after the self-hosted webfont loaded). Marks were then set at 18, 20, 22, 24 and 26 px against that wordmark. At cap height the block sits; above about 1.1 × cap it starts to shout down the serif. The rule that comes out of this — mark height = wordmark cap height — is therefore a number, not a preference.

Geometry was generated, not nudged. Every control point, angle and stroke width is a hand-made decision, but the arithmetic that turns a centreline into a band of even perpendicular weight, mitres the joins and clips to the square is done by tools/brand_marks.py. That is why the two arms of the recommended V carry the same optical weight despite sitting at different angles, and it is why re-running the script reproduces the marks byte for byte.

The six

Each one is a single idea. Where I cannot name the one detail that resolves the shape — its bite — I treat the mark as unfinished, not as subtle.

64 · 32 · 16

The Open Block

Recommended

The ideaA solid block of ink with a V cut out of it. The letter is never drawn — it is the paper showing through — and it leans, because its two arms sit at 12° and 43° from vertical rather than mirroring each other. The long arm does not stop at the edge of the block; it keeps going and takes the top-right corner with it. The block is the magnitude. The arm leaving is the heading.

Its biteThe missing corner. Without it, this is a letter parked in a box and the eye stops at the frame. With it, the block has been opened in one direction and the eye follows the arm out of the mark. It is also the detail that reads first at 16 px, which is the test of a real bite rather than a flourish.

Why it survives smallIt is a filled shape interrupted twice, and filled shapes are what survive at favicon size. Measured on a true 1 × raster it is legible down to 12 px and fully crisp from 14 px up. It is also the only candidate with real ink presence in a browser tab, where the neighbours are solid coloured squares and a thin glyph simply disappears.

64 · 32 · 16

Waterline

Survives 16 px · not recommended

The ideaThe publication's own 2 px masthead rule, taken off the horizontal and cut out of a block of ink. A level rule divides a page; a rule with a bearing is going somewhere. Nothing is added to the identity — the mark is made of a line the pages already print.

Its biteTwenty-one degrees of rise, which is the entire mark. That is also the honest problem with it: when the bite is the only thing there, there is nothing underneath it.

Why it survives small — and why I still reject itIt is the most robust silhouette of the six and unbreakable at 16 px. But it carries no trace of the name, it could belong to a great many things, and at a glance a block cut by a diagonal reads closer to a negation symbol than to a heading. Distinctive enough to invest meaning into is the bar; this clears legibility and misses that.

64 · 32 · 16

The Overshoot

Survives 16 px · not recommended

The ideaThe ancestor kept whole. A V drawn with Newsreader's stroke contrast, whose thin arm does not stop at the cap line but keeps rising — and in rising, absorbs the chevron that used to float above it in docs/favicon.svg. Two shapes become one shape, which is the only honest way to refine a mark made of two pieces.

Its biteThe overshoot itself. An arm that passes the cap line is a deliberate act; that is what separates a mark from a character someone set in a box.

Why it survives small — and why I still reject itIt survives only after the thin arm was thickened; at its first drawing the arm fell below one pixel at 16 px and dissolved. Legibility is no longer the objection. Weight is: in the lockup it reads as a stray glyph that fell out of the wordmark rather than a mark standing beside it, and it repeats a letter the wordmark already contains in Newsreader's own hand. A mark should not compete with the type it sits next to.

The Standard

Fails 16 px

The ideaThe slash from AI/Vector planted on the rule it stands above — the publication's signature diagonal given a ground to rise from, like a standard driven into a line.

Its biteThe rule running edge to edge, which turns a floating diagonal into something printed.

Why it failsAt 16 px the foot of the slash and the rule merge into a single dark smear, and what survives of the shape reads as a tick sitting on a line. Thickening both elements — which I tried — made it heavier without making it clearer, because the failure is the junction, not the weight. Two elements meeting at an acute angle is a shape that favicon size does not forgive. Recorded as drawn, tested and failed, so it is not attempted again.

64 · 32 · 16

Magnitude

Survives 16 px · not recommended

The ideaA vector reduced to its definition: a quantity that has a heading. One stroke, all its mass at the tail, nothing at the tip. Direction stated without an arrowhead anywhere on the page.

Its biteThe taper. It is what makes the stroke a vector rather than a line, and it sets which end is the front without drawing a head.

Why it survives small — and why I still reject itIt is perfectly clean at 16 px; the taper is not the problem. Beside the wordmark it reads as a tick, and a checkmark is the last thing a publication whose promise is curated, not aggregated wants to be saying — it implies verification the newsletter does not claim to perform. A mark that says the wrong thing clearly is worse than one that says nothing.

64 · 32 · 16

Caret & Rule

Fails 16 px

The ideaThe other half of the ancestor. Where The Overshoot keeps the V and absorbs the chevron, this keeps the chevron and retires the V: a heading set above the line, asymmetric so that it leans rather than pointing straight up.

Its biteThe off-centre apex with unequal arms. A symmetrical caret is a user-interface arrow; an asymmetric one is a mark.

Why it failsAt 16 px the apex blunts to a flat top and the arms fuse with the rule below, leaving something that reads as a roof or an eject glyph. This is the evidence behind a decision the brief left open: of the ancestor's two pieces, the V is the half worth keeping and the chevron is the half to retire. The chevron cannot carry the mark alone at the size the mark has to work at.

This is a rule falling out of a measurement rather than a taste. The footer wordmark is 19 px, its cap height is 12.75 px, and a 13 px mark is under the size at which this mark stays crisp. The recommendation, and the case against it

I recommend The Open Block. Here is the argument, followed by the four strongest attacks I could make on it and what each one cost.

Why this one

  • It keeps the ancestor without repeating the wordmark. The V survives from docs/favicon.svg, but as negative space — so it never competes with Newsreader's drawn letterforms the way a second, drawn V would.
  • It answers the name in one shape. A vector is magnitude and direction. The block is the magnitude; the arm leaving through the corner is the direction. No arrow is drawn anywhere.
  • It has the right ink weight for these pages. The issue template already prints 2 px solid ink rules above every section. A solid block belongs to that vocabulary; a thin glyph would look borrowed from a different publication.
  • It passes the memory test. "A dark square with a white V, corner sliced off" is a sentence a reader can sketch after seeing it once.
  • It is as good reversed as upright. Confirmed on the 16 px raster. That means one drawing serves light tabs, dark tabs, social cards and a future dark mode.

The case against, tested

"A V knocked out of a square is generic." Partly fair, and it was the objection I took most seriously. What makes this one specific is the lean and the sliced corner, so the test was whether both survive at 16 px — if they vanish, the mark really is generic where it matters most. On the raster both are visible at 16 px. The objection is answered by the pixels, not by argument.

"A solid block will overpower an airy serif wordmark." Tested rather than assumed, at 18, 20, 22, 24 and 26 px against the measured 21.81 px cap height. It does overpower — above roughly 1.1 × cap. At cap height it sits. That is exactly why the size relationship is written as a rule below and not left to whoever implements it.

"It reads as a tick." It did, at the first drawing, where the left arm sat 8° off vertical and the shape leaned hard enough to read as a checkmark. Six variants were rendered at 16–72 px and compared; at 12°/43° the left arm is far enough off vertical to read as an arm of a V, and at 18° the letter turns symmetrical and loses the lean entirely. The committed angles are the middle of that range.

"That is two ideas — a letter, and a cut corner." The charge would be fatal if true. I claim it is one: the corner is not a second gesture but the place where the letter's own arm leaves the block. Remove the V and there is no corner cut; remove the corner cut and the V has nowhere to go. Neither survives without the other, which is the test for whether two marks have become one.

On colour: one ink — but this one deserves an explicit ruling

I recommend the mark ships as a single ink, inheriting its colour with fill="currentColor". I am less certain of this than of anything else in the document, so here is the variant rather than a summary of it.

Ink block above, vermilion block below · both at 22 px, and both at 16 px in a tab

The vermilion block is a real option and I will not pretend otherwise: it has obvious ancestry in the Economist's red box, and at 16 px on a tab shelf it is the more distinctive of the two by a clear margin. What decides it against, for me, is the masthead: the accent is already spent on the slash in AI/Vector, and putting a vermilion block 14 px to its left means the eye meets the accent, then finds a second accent inside the word. The slash stops being the publication's one emphatic gesture and becomes an echo of the block. Vermilion is the only saturated identity colour the palette allows, and a colour used twice in 200 px of masthead is decoration rather than emphasis.

This is the one place where I would not be surprised to be overruled, and it is a clean either/or: the same path, one attribute different. There are no gradients, no glows and no third colour anywhere in this proposal either way.

4 · Favicon — docs/favicon.svg

Generated from the canonical path, never hand-edited. Two things about the current file need deciding when it is replaced, and both are observations rather than instructions:

  • The present favicon paints an opaque #ffffff rectangle behind the mark, which is a cold white against the warm paper the rest of the identity uses. The replacement should have a transparent ground so the browser's own tab colour shows through in both light and dark strips.
  • currentColor does not inherit inside a favicon — an SVG favicon renders as its own document, so currentColor resolves to the initial black. The generated file therefore needs an explicit fill plus a colour-scheme query, while the canonical mark keeps currentColor for in-page use:
<svg xmlns="http://www.w3.org/2000/svg" viewBox="0 0 100 100">
  <style>path{fill:#3b352d}
    @media (prefers-color-scheme:dark){path{fill:#f4f0e7}}</style>
  <path fill-rule="evenodd" d="…canonical path…"/>
</svg>

One thing found while reading the templates, unrelated to the mark: templates/issue.html.j2 line 23 pins the Safari mask-icon colour to #a83a2a, and the current docs/favicon.svg uses the same value. The ratified accent is oklch(0.49 0.135 30), which the template's own comment records as approximately #9f3b2e. Those are different vermilions. It has nothing to do with which mark is chosen, but it should be corrected in the same pass.

What this document does not establish

  • Nothing is applied and nothing is ratified. The working tree contains only docs/brand/** and tools/brand_marks.py. No template, no config, no favicon has been touched, and no commit has been made.
  • Rendering was verified in one engine. All rasters come from headless Chrome on macOS. Safari, Firefox and the various favicon caches were not tested, and fill-rule="evenodd" at 16 px is exactly the kind of thing that can differ between rasterisers.
  • Nothing was checked on a physical screen or in print. Every judgement here is from rasters at device scale factor 1, which is a good proxy for a standard display and a weaker one for a high-density laptop screen at a normal viewing distance.
  • No trademark or collision search was run. "Distinctive" in this document means distinctive against the other five candidates and against a handful of placeholder tab neighbours, not against a register.
  • The index breakpoint sizes are computed, not measured — see the note in section 2.
  • Where the canonical SVG should live so a fork can swap it is a contract question that touches config/brand.yaml and the render path. That is the architect's and release-engineer's call, not mine, and it is flagged rather than answered.
